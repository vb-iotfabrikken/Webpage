When a diocese first rolls out preservation-climate monitoring, the question
that comes up in every kick-off meeting is the same: _"What's the right
relative humidity for our church?"_ The answer, uncomfortably, is
_"it depends"_ — and the standard that tells you why is EN 15757.

## The core idea

EN 15757 breaks with the old fixed-setpoint approach. Instead of specifying
"organ lofts shall be at 50 % RH ± 5 %", it asks you to measure what the
object has historically experienced and stay within those bands. For a
Danish parish church whose organ has survived 200 winters at 30 % RH and 200
summers at 65 % RH, a new 50 % setpoint would actually _damage_ the
instrument.

## What we found

Across 60 parish churches monitored for 24 months, we found three consistent
patterns:

1. **Winter peaks under the gallery.** The cold nave pulls moisture
   downwards when services start, and the area under the organ gallery
   spikes 8–12 % RH above the average. This surprised no conservators but
   hadn't been quantified before.
2. **Sunday cycles stabilise in three hours.** Contrary to folk wisdom, a
   typical heating-for-services cycle returns to baseline RH within three
   hours of service end. The instrument movement stays within EN 15757
   bands in 94 % of cases.
3. **Vestry microclimates diverge.** Small, occasionally heated vestries
   attached to unheated naves showed the most erratic climate. They are
   always the first place to see damage, always the last to get sensors.

## What's next

The full data set underpins an ongoing joint study with the National Museum
of Denmark and DTU. Preliminary findings suggest we can predict damage
risk for altar-piece panels six months before visible cracking, just from
the moving 90-day RH band.
