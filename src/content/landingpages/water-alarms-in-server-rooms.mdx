A CRAC unit that dumps condensate onto the floor of a server room is rarely a matter of *if* — it's a matter of *when*. And when it happens at 3 a.m. on a Saturday, response time is the single most important thing you've got.

## What the CRAC unit's own sensors miss

Most server rooms have one sensor built into the CRAC unit. It covers **the unit's own drip tray**. It doesn't catch:

- Condensation on overhead pipes.
- Leaks from fire valves and sprinkler installations.
- Water that gets in from outside via floor penetrations.
- A leak in a UPS cooling loop.
- A thaw that hits a rooftop server room.

That's why we always recommend a dedicated detection solution **on top of** whatever the equipment itself provides.

## Typical sensor plan

For a medium-sized server room (10–20 racks):

1. One sensor under each CRAC unit.
2. One sensor per row under the raised floor.
3. One sensor by the door and at pipe penetrations.
4. One sensor under UPS battery cabinets.
5. One sensor at a low point where water pools if anything slips in.

Every sensor is shown on a floor plan, so NOC staff can see **exactly** where the alarm is triggered — not just "there's water in the server room".

## Integration patterns we see

- **ServiceNow / Jira / TOPdesk** — automatic P1 ticket when an alarm is triggered.
- **[MQTT to the NOC](/en/articles/mqtt-integration/)** — real-time data into your existing monitoring.
- **Phone tree** — if the on-call person doesn't acknowledge within X minutes, the next one is called.
- **Email to the insurance broker** — to document the response time in the event of a claim.

## What does downtime cost?

Our customers in the data-centre segment have told us that even five minutes of extra response time easily costs a six-figure sum in SLA refunds. Seen in that light, the investment in dedicated water detection is negligible.
